INSANEv0.2 Rebuilt after a 30-day diary study · no cash accepted

I used v0.1 every day for a month. It didn't die of addiction. It died of loneliness.

Playing a real user exposed what the first design ignored: two friends is not a network, nobody has something to swap every day, and three separate handoffs always fail. v0.2 rebuilds the app around friend circles, a daily shared dare, a favor board, and one weekly Market Day. The simulator is now checked against published retention benchmarks and two real barter-currency collapses before it compares anything. v0.1 is still in the repo under prototype/v1/.

What happened in this run

    Every change in v0.2 traces back to a day in the diary where v0.1 broke. Nothing was added without a moment that demanded it. The last column puts the old v0.1 mechanic back into v0.2, one at a time, and shows what the simulator says happens (3-seed average).

    v0.2 changeDiary moment it fixesWhat it replacesSimulator, with the old mechanic back

    Kept from v0.1

    • Dares as payment, with stakes people actually want. Day 4 was the best day of the v0.1 month.
    • Multi-person loop matching, now run in one batch per week.
    • The Insanity Index, now weekly per circle, scored on absurdity and creativity, with danger removing reward.
    • The monthly jubilee.

    Where the simulator disagrees with the diary

    Putting Trust decay back into v0.2 raises simulated day-30 retention and swap volume. Once a circle is dense, loss aversion does exactly what it is designed to do: it gets people to open the app. The model counts opens; it does not count how it felt to open. In the diary, decay was the first thing that felt like a trick (day 2) and it locked the user out of the one feature they wanted (day 12). v0.2 leaves it out on purpose. The real-world test should settle it: run one circle with decay and two without, and compare day-14 posting against the “felt like a punishment” count.

    New risks v0.2 introduces

    • Daily Dare fatigue. BeReal lost an estimated 60% of daily users within months of its peak. Circle-written prompts slow the decay; they do not stop it.
    • Peer pressure in a small circle. Fewer strangers, more people whose opinion matters.
    • Market Day needs one city. Long-distance friends only get favors.
    • Circles churn together. If a circle dies, everyone in it leaves at once.

    This version follows a four-question method: say what a top professional would do that wasn't asked for, name the rawest material, name the methods, and say what other than the designer would show the result is right. Then rewrite the prompt to ask for exactly that.

    1 · What a pro would do that wasn't asked

    • Find the atomic network: the smallest group where the app works on day one.
    • Check frequency. People have something to swap monthly; the app needs a daily reason.
    • Design the physical handoff, not just the match.
    • Pick a north-star metric that isn't DAU: completed exchanges per circle per week.
    • Test in a group chat before building, with pass/fail set in advance.

    2 · The rawest material

    • Argentina's trueque clubs, 2001–02: about 2.5 million people at the peak; new members got 50 free créditos; counterfeiting spread; collapse by the end of 2002.
    • Bunz BTZ, 2018–19: 1,000 free tokens per user, 130M+ in circulation, 200+ Toronto shops; supply expanded, then abruptly scaled back in 2019.
    • Retention benchmarks: social apps keep roughly 25–29% on D1, 9–16% on D7, 4–10% on D30.
    • BeReal: 15–20M daily users in late 2022, about 6M by spring 2023.
    • A day-by-day diary of one user.

    3 · Methods and tools

    • Diary study and cognitive walkthrough.
    • Fogg Behavior Model (B = MAP) for each daily action.
    • Cohort retention curves and the smile curve.
    • Atomic-network analysis for cold start.
    • Liquidity math and cycle matching (kidney exchange).
    • Mutual-credit rules: limits, no free issuance (WIR, LETS).
    • Agent-based model: calibrate, reproduce history, then compare; average over seeds.

    4 · What shows it's right, other than us

    • Benchmarks: the no-hooks version must land in the published range first. See the checks on the Simulator tab.
    • History: free welcome credits must reproduce the trueque/Bunz collapse.
    • Real people: a two-week concierge test with three friend circles, judged against thresholds set in advance.
    • A stranger's pre-mortem.

    The rewritten prompt

    1. Be a real user. Play one realistic person for 30 days on the current prototype. Log minutes, what triggered each open, what I got, what annoyed me, and the day I'd quit. Shorter runs for a performer, a trader, and a lurker.
    2. Start from raw material: post-mortems of the trueque clubs, Bunz BTZ and WIR; published D1/D7/D30 benchmarks; BeReal's rise and fall.
    3. Use these methods: diary study, B = MAP, cohort retention, atomic network, liquidity math, mutual-credit rules.
    4. Make the simulator earn trust: calibrate a no-hooks version to benchmarks, reproduce the trueque/Bunz collapse, and only then compare v0.1 with v0.2.
    5. Redesign only what broke, naming the diary moment for every change.
    6. End with a two-week real-world test with pass/fail thresholds set in advance. Say so plainly if it doesn't work.
